M15 — A War That Should Not Be
R35 | Expanded production proposal. Catalog: Gate Fields; age fifteen investigates retaliation, escorts
civilians and can prevent a staged incident; the greater war escalates regardless. Age eighteen holds the
same terrain against external invaders. Next M16. All new dialogue, unnamed supporting actors, local
incidents and geometry are proposed. M14 established conflicting reports following the envoy's
assassination; neither culprit nor divine intermediary is confirmed. S29 and other optional investigations
enrich dialogue, not progression gates.

Scene 1 — The road has changed
Age fifteen. The Gate Fields were once a place where traders stopped to water animals before entering the
border districts. The road passes a shallow stone culvert, a split ridge and a small bell tower that signals when
a crossing is open. Today a wagon stands abandoned with one wheel in the mud, and soldiers from different
directions have raised temporary barriers. A farmer is turning a sign away from the road because its ordinary
destination arrow now points toward a closed crossing. These changes should be experienced through free
movement, not a narrated explanation. The player recognizes an ordinary geography becoming dangerous
before seeing a battle.

The warden stands by the crossing rather than at the head of a military force. He has permission to keep a
civilian route open but no authority to settle the empires' grievances. His assistant uses chalk on a slate to
record how many people have reached shelter. A bell rings once and then stops when a soldier orders the
bell keeper down. The silence is the first major sound cue.

Hero: “I've seen this road burning.”

Warden: “The armies weren't here yesterday.”

Hero: “No. I don't mean these armies.”

Warden: “Then tell me what you see now. We need to get people across.”

If M14's public statement was selected, the warden has heard that no culprit was verified; if private sharing
was chosen, he has been told only that inquiries continue. If civilian access was prioritized, he acknowledges
the request but explains that field conditions have worsened. Do not override M14's choice or make it
responsible for the war. The player receives a route map of three civilian gathering points and the shelter,
not a faction allegiance prompt.

Scene 2 — A reason to retaliate
A messenger says a supply wagon was allegedly attacked by a rival patrol. Near the west checkpoint a
damaged cart has been pushed sideways into the road, its load scattered, and a faction-colored pennant lies
under one wheel. A frightened driver disputes the messenger's description but cannot identify who moved


the cart. The player investigates a public scene without recreating the assault. Inspect the cart's cargo tally,
the pennant's mismatched issue marker and the driver statement; these fictional clues support a conclusion
that the displayed incident does not establish the alleged attacker, not a real-world forensic lesson or a
universal conspiracy proof.

Driver: “It was empty when they brought it here.”

Messenger: “They told me there were supplies on it.”

Hero: “Who told you?”

Messenger: “I only carried the message.”

At the junction a second crowd gathers around a rumored threat. Two officers are considering sending a
patrol across the neutral road, where an angry confrontation could begin. The hero can present the
contradictions to the warden, ask the driver to repeat their account from a safe position, or ask both officers
to inspect the official cargo list before anyone moves. Each approach uses the same evidence threshold: two
independent core observations and the driver's report. With insufficient evidence the warden marks the
claim unresolved and orders the civilian lane moved. If the player leaves, the confrontation still advances
only at an authored checkpoint, not by a hidden real-time timer. A return opportunity remains before the
evacuation begins.

If the threshold is met, the local officers agree to stand apart and the warden reroutes the wagon. No one
confesses to arranging the scene. If the player elects evacuation first or declines to intervene, the disputed
junction closes; a distant alarm announces the lost crossing and the player follows the alternate route. The
story records only the local outcome, not a claim that the player prevented war or caused it. Optional S29
evidence can prompt one extra line about why the warden refuses to endorse the accusation; it is not a
substitute for the core scene observations.

Warden (if persuaded): “Nobody crosses that line until somebody can tell me what happened.”

Hero: “There are people waiting behind us.”

Warden: “Then that's where we start.”

Scene 3 — The people without an army
The player accompanies three small civilian groups through the Gate Fields: a family waiting by the culvert, a
merchant with a damaged cart and two travelers separated from their guide. Each group is identifiable by
ordinary possessions and voice rather than by faction color. An injured civilian can choose to walk with
assistance or rest at a staffed intermediate shelter while a helper accompanies the player. This is a choice
about the logistics of escape, not a test of whose life is valuable.

Refugee: “Please, get us out.”

Hero: “Can you reach the wall if I stay with you?”

Refugee: “If my daughter can see me.”

There are two authored routes: a broad farm lane with a guarded crossing and a raised service path beyond
the culvert. Both lead to the same protected receiving yard. The farm lane has more cover and space for carts
but closes if the disputed incident is unresolved. The service path is always usable by all roles, with a short
bypass ramp for a damaged bridge. Player can negotiate passage, help clear a stalled wheel through a simple


interact action, or escort the walking group first and return with a warden's helper. Optional hostile
encounters use fictional aggressors already established by the scene; no allied NPC can become hostile solely
because of faction affiliation. Neither route requires a special skill, glide or relic. NPCs stop safely if the player
strays, and return markers guide the player back without teleporting civilians into hazards.

At the shelter the assistant updates the slate for each group that actually arrives. A missing group is marked
waiting at the last safe checkpoint, not dead by default. The player can recover an interrupted escort before
completion. On the final return the player can see two separated military lines on the distant ridge; their
presence does not make the shelter unsafe. Reports announce retaliatory mobilizations beyond the fields
even if the player prevented the local confrontation.

Merchant: “You got our cart through.”

Hero: “I couldn't keep the road open.”

Merchant: “You kept us on it.”

Scene 4 — The same bend, beneath another sky
At the staffed shelter, an authored migration becomes available through a stable narrative beat. The
transition is brief and uses the sound of a bell being struck, cut off by the scrape of a barricade. Age eighteen
awakens standing near the comparable culvert in the original reality. He is not reliving the younger hero's
evacuation. This place is currently being overrun by creatures from outside humanity, and the older hero acts
on his own surroundings with his existing abilities and gear. Keep the current age-specific equipment
snapshot and independent NPC state.

The familiar split ridge is visible beyond a ruined roofline. The culvert is deeper with a repaired stone lip; the
bell tower's upper timbers are gone. The silhouettes on the horizon are visibly nonhuman, and their
movements differ from the earlier armies' orderly lines. Use actual environment changes rather than a filter
or clone of the preserved field. The shared geography explains why people converge on the same refuge
without implying that both wars have the same human origin.

Guard: “We have a lane open for the wounded. If it closes, we go through the lower yard.”

Hero: “The culvert. There is a way around the broken bridge.”

Guard: “You know this place?”

Hero: “I know where the road goes.”

The player can scout a safe route before joining a defensive hold. The guard directs a civilian group toward
shelter, while the player protects a marked retreat lane. In the first encounter, a small number of invading
creatures threaten the outer fence; use clearly signaled arrivals, broad combat space, and no attacks inside
the shelter. Damage-role attacks, ranged attacks, or shield-based protection are equally valid, with guards
handling targets outside a player's chosen role. The second encounter occurs at the broken crossing while
the last civilians move to cover. The player may hold until the group is safe or take the lower bypass and
coordinate with the guard. No mandatory kill quota or untelegraphed reinforcement behind the player.
Difficulty and numbers remain tuning parameters, not final values. Death restores the checkpoint and group
state; it does not erase previously saved shelter arrivals or duplicate rewards.


Scene 5 — What survives a burning road
When the final civilian group reaches the receiving yard, the older hero takes a moment beside a damaged
signpost. Beneath the sign is the same shape of stone step the younger hero passed. The player can inspect it
and hear a civilian ask for water. It is an ordinary sentence that the player heard earlier in a different voice.
Do not use the same NPC model, voice or name to imply a confirmed reincarnation. The older hero responds
by handing the civilian to a shelter attendant, not by making an exposition speech.

Civilian: “Is there water inside?”

Hero: “Yes. Go with her. I'll stay until the others arrive.”

A short, optional overlook shows the road behind them burning. The fire is beyond the defended route; the
player cannot put out the entire invasion. The original-world guard thanks the hero for securing this
particular passage, then begins the next ordinary task. The older protagonist notices that people instinctively
selected the same path toward shelter. His journal records the geography, the creatures, and the evacuation
separately from the meaning he suspects.

Scene 6 — A war that should not be
Return through the authored shelter checkpoint in the preserved world. Smoke from a distant human conflict
hangs above the ridge; the local shelter remains intact. A representative delivers conflicting reports of fresh
mobilizations. The hero can report the staged incident as disputed, the local crossing closure or reopening,
and the civilians' safe arrival. Sable Vey's reply arrives as a short message or in person at a safe hub,
according to the actual travel implementation. She does not claim that the reports prove a goddess's
involvement.

Hero: “They were fighting something that wasn't human. Here we're doing this to each other.”

Warden: “Who is ‘they’?”

Hero: “I wish I knew how to explain it.”

Warden: “You don't have to explain it to keep the next road open.”

The final choice determines the emphasis of the outgoing report: document the uncertain accusation;
request more neutral shelters; or ask that independent witness statements be preserved. These are
compatible requests and none guarantees military compliance. The last option may foreshadow M16's
detained witnesses, but no witness is detained in M15 merely to unlock the next mission. A messenger
reports that a soldier in a border town has begun describing an invasion nobody there remembers. The
player receives M16 — When the Forgotten Speak. Journal: “The roads match. The enemies do not. I saw
people reach shelter in both places. I cannot tell why the preserved world is becoming so much like the other
one.”

Developer-only arc: the unnamed hostile goddess wants suffering in the preserved reality to converge
toward the conditions of the condemned reality, helping her reopen it. M15 depicts the observable human
consequences, not the hidden mechanism or culprit. Her strategy does not erase ordinary human agency. Do
not show her physically directing the staged incident, establish a specific accomplice, or confirm that the
assassination and incident share an organizer. No early Celestial Sight or finalized reincarnation explanation.

D39 — The Bow Above the Marsh
R35 | Catalog: eastern marsh; three reeds and a leaning tower form a bow at age fifteen; broken lookout
curved beam and two braces align at age eighteen; paired focus reveals submerged hunter hide accessible
via dry ledge; recover a personal bow, bow unlock or cosmetic equivalent; free roam. “My fingers know the


draw.” Expanded proposal, not built. No mandatory connection to M15 or S29, no premature Celestial Sight,
no player-controlled migration.

Scene 1 — A shape in the reeds
Age fifteen. An eastern marsh lies below a low ridge where a leaning lookout tower rises above the reeds. Its
base is partly surrounded by shallow water, but a raised bank runs around the wettest area. The traveler can
approach on foot from an ordinary path or land on a broad upper meadow if gliding is already unlocked. The
discovery is not signposted by a floating quest icon. Three unusually straight reed stems frame the silhouette
of the tower's bowed edge; a nearby hunter studies the wet ground without noticing their relationship.

Hunter: “The marsh looks flat from the road. It isn't.”

Hero: “Was there a tower here before?”

Hunter: “Before my time. The bank still holds if you stay on the stones.”

At a flat bank shoulder, the player can aim ordinary paired focus toward three reed landmarks and the tower
edge. The upright reeds supply the imagined bowstring and the tower's lean suggests the curved limb. The
arrangement uses depth and perspective, not exact pixel matching. Wind animates surrounding foliage, but
the clue's structural base stays stable. Provide hold or toggle, an optional widened tolerance and guidance
via shape/caption rather than color alone. Register D39_F15 once the player holds a valid alignment. One
alignment alone does not open the hide. The hero says, “That's a strange shape,” not “That's my bow.”

Scene 2 — The lookout that fell
Age eighteen. The same marsh shelf supports a damaged lookout beside a guarded path. Its curved support
beam rests between two angled braces. The older hero already carries a personal bow or, if loadout differs,
has access to an established personal weapon stored at the lookout. The player inspects the familiar grip
while a guard checks the far bank. Do not force an equip change, fabricate a new combat unlock for the older
hero, or remove an existing weapon to create the mystery.

Guard: “That old support still keeps the roof from falling.”

Hero: “It's curved like a limb.”

Guard: “A bow?”

Hero: “I think so.”

The player aligns the curved beam and both braces from a protected viewing pad. Register D39_F18. An
optional quiet sound of a bowstring accompanies completion; captions and a visual outline communicate
success with sound disabled. The older hero continues his own patrol or exploration. Both flags can be
acquired in either order across available narrative migrations; the player need not complete M15, and no
side quest is generated automatically. No timer or loss of progress on return.

Scene 3 — Under the waterline
When both flags are valid, the younger protagonist can return to the bank and use focus again. A concealed
access seam becomes visible in the tower's stone base. A dry ledge remains above the shallow water and
leads around the base to an entrance beneath the old waterline. The player never has to dive or perform a
frame-perfect jump. The opening becomes enterable only after collision, interior and safe exit are ready.


Once occupied, an interior lease keeps the passage navigable even if focus is released; a simple internal latch
opens egress independently. The wet outer stone should contrast with the surprisingly dry vaulted interior,
suggesting a real protected hunter hide rather than a floating portal room.

The first chamber contains a broken stool, empty arrow rack, worn boot scraper and a small drainage grate.
A narrow direct passage has a damaged hinged rack which creaks before swinging into its empty resting
position. The player can wait for the movement to finish or take the wide drainage gallery around it. The
mechanism is visible and resettable, never an invisible damage zone. An optional alcove contains an ordinary
hunting tally that describes changing water levels but does not name an ancient civilization or divine enemy.
The main relic chamber is a dry niche with a low bench, wall hooks and a bow resting in a lined case. Its grip
is worn asymmetrically where a thumb would settle. The hero lifts it and briefly tests its familiar balance
without firing at a person.

Hero: “My fingers know the draw.”

The older hero, at the corresponding lookout, briefly pauses while adjusting his own bow. He notices the
same grip position and returns to his existing task. This is an authored sensory correspondence, not a forced
meeting or item transfer. Keep older inventory, ammunition and progression unchanged. Proposed younger
reward: bow unlock if appropriate to progression; if the bow role or equivalent is already unlocked, provide a
non-stat cosmetic personal bow or archival collectible under existing inventory rules. Never replace stronger
equipped gear automatically. A safe practice target may let the player test the normal bow controls,
including charge and release, but the target is optional and not a new combat mechanic. No reward gated
behind a headshot or precision timing.

Scene 4 — The marsh from above
After inspecting the relic, the player walks a broad ramp toward a protected lookout inside the tower base.
The same three reeds and bowed tower edge are visible through a slit above the waterline. A second focus
alignment lets the player see the distinctive geometry from the corresponding older lookout angle. A quiet
breath and bowstring sound can suggest a sensory echo, but no spectral duplicate or explanatory timeline
appears. The younger hero wonders how his hands recognized a tool in a place he had never visited. Journal:
“The shape above the marsh matched the broken lookout. The bow's grip feels familiar. I do not know why.”
Complete once the relic and second vista are recorded; exit via latch and safe bank. Revisit remains possible;
no repeat grant.

S29 — Three Accounts

Scene 1 — An inn without banners
Age fifteen. A neutral border inn remains open to travelers regardless of allegiance. Its common room is
divided by a central hearth and a long table where maps can be unrolled without blocking passage. Sable Vey
is present during a safe story interval or can receive the evidence through a designated inn clerk if the active
main quest has her elsewhere. This avoids duplicating named characters or contradicting M14's hall
sequence. The clerk places three plain folders on the table; none bears a faction crest. A ledger lists which
documents the traveler has voluntarily supplied.

Sable: “What can you prove?”

Hero: “The stories disagree with the objects.”

Sable: “Then we must keep asking.”

The player may start S29 before gathering any evidence. The journal lists leads to the three cases but does
not auto-accept them, reveal their outcomes, or demand all three. With fewer than two completed sources,
Sable explains what is missing and the player can leave. An optional map marker points to a known
investigation contact; undiscovered locations remain discoverable through ordinary travel. No experience
reward for merely opening the table.

Scene 2 — Three folders, three distinct limits
The evidence table presents one card per verified source, with three fields: observed or documented,
witness-reported, and unresolved interpretation. For S26, the proposed card records the seal/register


mismatch and forgery conclusion only once the source quest validates it; it cannot establish who
commissioned the forgery or prove an empire responsible. For S27, the recorded gate condition and
payment report establish a local sabotage case with unnamed payer; they cannot identify who funded
another case. For S28, the observed stitching mismatch and issue ledger undermine the planted banner
claim; the actual issued banner may still be missing. Never combine the three into a single culprit just
because all are suspicious.

For each submitted source the player chooses a reasonable conclusion from accessible text. If they select an
unsupported accusation, Sable points to the missing connection and invites reconsideration without
punishment. A correct response does not require knowing a faction color or reading tiny prop text. When any
two valid source IDs are recorded, Sable accepts a joint statement: the examined incidents do not support
the broad accusations made from them. If a third is available, it corroborates the danger of treating symbols
as proof but does not magically confirm a shared plot. The same completion threshold applies to all pairs.
S26 remains separately pending for its full script, so the source interface must fail closed rather than
inventing evidence if that quest is not implemented.

Sable: “A seal is not an order. A banner is not a witness. A payment does not tell us who paid.”

Hero: “Then what do we tell them?”

Sable: “What we can demonstrate, and what we cannot.”

Scene 3 — The accounts of another war
Age eighteen. In a corresponding shelter building, a recorder has collected independent statements about
the external invasion. The older hero can inspect three short, clearly attributed accounts: a traveler saw
shapes descend beyond the ridge; a shelter worker heard people calling for help by the crossing; a guard saw
creatures breach a defensive lane. These statements are proposed fictional records. They concern different
observations, not identical wording or a common human attacker. The player can compare them with the
older hero's own experiences without declaring that any one account is perfect. An optional audio reading
and transcription make the records accessible.

Recorder: “They don't agree about where it began.”

Hero: “They weren't standing in the same place.”

Recorder: “That's why I wrote down where they were.”

The older hero leaves the statements with their author and records a summary in his own journal. The player
may complete this perspective before or after the younger investigation through available migrations. No
fabricated documents pass between worlds. No literal transfer of papers, no forced meeting and no use of a
faction emblem to imply a shared origin for both wars. S29's younger completion relies on two valid faction
records; the older reading is a linked perspective milestone that can be viewed independently, not a gate
that forces migration before M15.

Scene 4 — A statement with room for doubt
Sable offers to send a short statement to the representatives or keep it available to future travelers. Either
choice documents verified findings and unresolved questions; the actual distribution is recorded so later
dialogue does not falsely claim public release. The hero may request a neutral civilian route recommendation
as an attached note. If M15 has already ended, the statement becomes an archival record and does not


rewind the local incident or the war. If M15 is still pending, it unlocks one additional contextual exchange
with the warden, never mandatory access.

Hero: “Will they listen?”

Sable: “Some will. The rest may need to read it twice.”

A third case submitted after completion is attached as an addendum, with a distinct acknowledgment and no
duplicate reward. The journal records source IDs and the player's actual choice, not a predetermined
accusation. Proposed normal optional XP or journal milestone once. No faction trust penalty for an
unsupported initial choice, no unique equipment, no automatic completion of S26–S28 or D39. Older account
milestone remains inspectable even after younger completion.

M16 — When the Forgotten Speak
R36 | Expanded production proposal. Required catalog beats: border town; detained soldier remembers
creatures descending; the familiar family recovers an impossible memory; age eighteen verifies events at the
devastated counterpart; protect or release witnesses through evidence. M15 is prerequisite. S40 and D27
were expanded together in R29 and remain optional, independently completable. All added NPC roles,
dialogue, dimensions and quest mechanics below are proposed, not new approved canon. M17 retains the
revelation of deliberate historical alignment.

Scene 1 — A bell that nobody rings
Age fifteen. The border town's market terrace overlooks the same chimney gaps used in D27. Human soldiers
now stand at the entrances, but the streets are still inhabited: a baker carries a tray inside, a child folds a
paper boat, and a municipal worker pulls a barricade away from a doorway so a resident can enter. The
player should see a community struggling to continue its ordinary life, not a newly abandoned combat arena.
The wind swings a bell above the street, and for a moment the protagonist hears the interrupted crossing
bell from M15 in memory. No physical bell strikes. A caption distinguishes a remembered sound from a
present-world sound.

The warden receives the hero at a public notice board. A temporary order asks guards to take reports of
creatures above the roofs to a municipal interview room; some witnesses are being held there pending
review. The warden considers the order excessive, but has not yet gathered enough evidence to challenge it.
The player may ask who authorized it, why the witnesses are frightened, or where the people are being held.
The answer establishes administrative responsibility for the detention, not responsibility for the supernatural
events.

Warden: “They're saying the town has been attacked. Look around. It hasn't.”

Hero: “The road was burning before the armies came. I remember it.”

Warden: “Then perhaps you should hear what they're saying before I put another name on that list.”

The route splits at the board. The mandatory objectives are to inspect one witness account, visit the familiar
family and examine the municipal shelter. The order is flexible. A separate S40 marker may appear only if its
optional story is available; completing M16 will neither silently award S40 nor reset its existing outcome. If
S40 was completed, its soldier can appear in safe lodging, the courtyard or a private consultation, according
to the recorded resolution and consent. Otherwise the mandatory interview can be conducted with that
soldier at the public-facing portion of the guardroom, without consuming the S40 private interview. His story
is acknowledged as an observation, not accepted wholesale.


Scene 2 — The remembered sky
The soldier sits with his coat folded, watching the rooftop instead of the interviewer. Through the window
the player sees intact tiles and a chimney. He describes a dark shape descending above a broken roof, a silver
light and people taking cover behind a wall. The protagonist recognizes the evacuation geometry, but does
not yet know whether this description matches his own experience or a second person's.

Soldier: “We've lost this town before.”

Hero: “There are no creatures here.”

Soldier: “I know. I walked the street this morning. I kept looking for the roof that wasn't there.”

The player can ask about the light, the shelter or what the soldier remembers immediately afterward. The
first two produce usable environmental observations; the third reveals the uncertainty of his recollection. If
S40 consent allows private sharing, the trusted account can populate the observation card, skipping repeated
questions. If consent prohibits sharing his name, the interface presents an anonymous environmental
description instead. If S40 was declined, a municipal lookout reports the existence of similar accounts
without identifying the soldier. No player is forced to reopen an optional private testimony.

The journal distinguishes three layers: observed present conditions, reported memory and independently
corroborated older-world events. It must not say “this witness lived a previous life” or “this person is a
migrant.” Before corroboration, the player may tell the warden the account is internally coherent, but cannot
declare it verified. The game teaches this distinction by keeping separate columns, not through a long tutorial
popup.

Scene 3 — The family and the dripping water
The preserved-world family introduced in M12 remains distinct from Mira, Renn and Tovin of M11. Reuse the
established family NPC IDs, names and relationships from M12 rather than assigning new ones. Their normal
domestic routine is interrupted when a family member hears water dripping from a repaired gutter. They
stop speaking, then describe a subterranean shelter, wooden slats, a damp cup shelf and a song. The player
recognizes parts of the mining refuge from M11. The memory is incomplete and may combine sensory
details; it does not establish that this family and the original family are literally the same people.

Villager: “I remember the water dripping underground.”

Hero: “Were you alone?”

Villager: “No. Someone was holding my hand. I remember being afraid to let go.”

Hero: “Do you remember who?”

Villager: “I remember my family here. Isn't that enough?”

The player may listen without questioning further, ask whether they want their account recorded, or offer to
inspect the shelter independently. Each path preserves the family's right to decline disclosure. The
mandatory evidence is the volunteered description of water, wood and the cup shelf; a private memory of
the hand is never required. A small environmental interaction lets the hero adjust the loose gutter with a
household member. The task returns the family to a practical present-world activity rather than using the
memory to take away their agency. If the M12 meal was accepted, use a familiar greeting; if declined, use a
neutral welcome, not a trust penalty.


The family has prepared an ordinary meal. The hero can remain briefly, but a journal objective points to the
municipal shelter. When the player leaves, the family continues its routine and does not freeze in a
distressed pose. No combat encounter interrupts this domestic scene. The player may return to talk after the
chapter, when the family can describe the current day's activities instead of repeating the frightening
memory indefinitely.

Scene 4 — The room below the town
The municipal shelter is a functioning cellar beneath the market, unrelated to the hidden balcony entrance of
D27. Its keeper shows the hero a recent inventory: blankets, spare timber, water containers. Inspect the
entrance direction, intact rafters and an ordinary cistern. These observations establish that the remembered
broken roof and emergency refuge conditions are absent from the present town. The player should
understand that the absence of destruction is evidence against a literal present-day attack, not evidence that
the witnesses are lying.

A resident asks whether they will be detained for remembering something similar. The warden promises to
review the order, but cannot responsibly guarantee the authorities' future behavior. The hero can help
establish a private consultation point in the cellar's adjacent courtyard. The player moves a sign to mark a
clear exit, asks the keeper for a quiet room and assigns a trusted attendant from existing eligible NPCs. All
three interactions are safe and accessible without a combat build. The arrangement is available before the
older-world investigation so witnesses are not required to wait in distress for a speculative theory.

Keeper: “I have room for people. I don't have room for another argument.”

Hero: “Then let's make it a place where they don't have to argue.”

At the staffed rest point, the authored age transition becomes available. It is a narrative migration of the
playable protagonist only. The player can finish current interactions and save before accepting it. A short
sound bridge uses a dripping gutter becoming a mine drip and a distant evacuation bell becoming audible;
the player returns to control with the older hero's own equipment and objectives. No witness crosses with
him.

Scene 5 — A town that really fell
Age eighteen. The corresponding market has collapsed sections of roof, broken chimney edges and a guarded
evacuation passage. Reuse the surveyed geography from D27/S40, but do not require completion of either
activity. A survivor guide brings the player to a ruined direction board and a wall where civilians previously
sheltered. The player inspects the broken roof silhouette and a silver evacuation lamp from a safe stair
landing. If S40 already recorded the observations, the journal may show them as previously known, but the
main scene still requires a short contextual conversation confirming the location is current and accessible.

Survivor guide: “The lamp told us when the street was clear.”

Hero: “People were waiting behind this wall?”

Survivor guide: “Yes. We moved them in groups. There are fewer of us working the crossing now.”

The guide can show a recorded evacuation report or describe the event directly. The report confirms the
local signal and a creature incursion, not a named attacker, destiny or cosmic cause. From the terrace,
players may see distant creatures beyond the defended perimeter. A small optional exterior engagement
protects an arriving supply cart; the required evidence path remains out of combat. If the optional encounter


is active, the guide waits at the protected wall and returns to the same position after the player's recovery.
The original-world people remain active in their own crisis rather than acting as exhibits for the younger
hero's investigation.

The hero then visits the M11 refuge through its ordinary route or a staffed transfer checkpoint if distant
terrain is not available. Do not move the mine into the border town merely to shorten the quest. Revisit the
vent and the family shelf: the previous repair remains present, the medicine delivery remains complete, and
the familiar song can be heard only if appropriate to the family schedule. A steward or family member
voluntarily confirms the practical details of the refuge. The younger family's account resembles the physical
setting, but neither family is told it is a duplicate of the other. No identifying personal record crosses the
realities.

Hero: “The boards. The water. The shelf.”

Steward: “We moved the cup so it wouldn't fall into the drainage channel.”

Hero: “Someone described this place to me. They shouldn't have known it.”

Steward: “Perhaps someone who passed through told them.”

The older hero does not claim a supernatural explanation to the steward. He records specific observations
and returns to a safe rest location. The chapter establishes corroboration of environmental details, not proof
of the memory mechanism. The player has now directly witnessed why the younger hero's questions matter.

Scene 6 — The hearing without a verdict on identity
Return to age fifteen. The warden convenes a short administrative review in the municipal office, not a public
trial. The player can present a present-world survey, the independent older-world comparison and a consent-
limited witness summary. The warden is not asked to accept a different reality as a legal fact; he is asked to
acknowledge that an unusual account alone does not establish harmful conduct. The older observations may
guide the player's interpretation, but public release is justified through present evidence and lack of a
verified threat, not inaccessible proof that the office cannot inspect.

Warden: “We have no damaged roof, no bodies in the street and no report that these people harmed
anyone.”

Hero: “Then why are they being held?”

Clerk: “Because the order told us to review the reports.”

Hero: “Review the reports. Let the people go.”

Three practical resolutions are offered: approve safe release after the review, arrange a trusted private
account with a voluntary follow-up, or provide anonymous safe lodging for people who fear the crowd. The
latter two are voluntary, not detention under a new name. Named S40 outcomes remain intact; if the soldier
was already released, the scene instead concerns the remaining generic witnesses and confirms his earlier
result. The player may ask that names be removed from the public notice. This is a separate consent-safe
decision, not a morality score. The record shows who agreed to what and does not publish private family
details.

Outside, a villager returns to the market. One of the guards carries the temporary interview notice indoors.
The human war has not ended, and authorities in other towns may respond differently. The local change


matters because the player helped real people rather than because a cosmological theory was proven. The
warden gives the hero a lead: the ridge observatory holds old sky records and reports of unusual sightlines.
M17 opens only after M16's local protection objective has resolved.

Hero: “I thought I was the only one who remembered.”

Warden: “You don't have to be the only one who listens.”

Journal: “What they described exists somewhere I have been. I cannot say why they remember it. I can say
they should not be punished for remembering.” No goddess identity, Celestial Sight or final cosmological
explanation is revealed. The third reality remains outside this chapter's playable geography.

D40 — The Twin Blades of the Dune
R36 | Independent paired discovery. Catalog: Tameri desert escarpment, two rock fins and a far obelisk form
crossed blades at age fifteen; age eighteen aligns fallen barricades at supply camp; hidden sandstone cavern
contains twin training blades in worn case. Proposed dual blade technique or equivalent role reward. Neither
M16 nor S26 gates discovery. The older hero retains his own existing equipment. All new spatial details and
reward tuning remain proposals.

Scene 1 — The stones that cross
At fifteen the hero follows a trade path along a desert escarpment. The sandstone is layered, pale ochre
above deep rust-colored seams, with wind-smoothed faces interrupted by broken angular edges. Two tall
rock fins stand at different distances, and a distant obelisk rises between them. At most angles they appear
unrelated; from a broad ledge their contours overlap into the silhouette of crossed blades. The player may
approach on foot from the caravan trail, or glide to a marked shelf away from the edge. An ordinary shade
shelter and drinking-water prop make the approach a believable resting place even if the player ignores the
discovery.

Caravan scout: “The wind takes sand from that ledge every season.”

Hero: “Those stones look like something.”

Scout: “From here? Just stones.”

On the focus shelf, a generous sightline cone allows the player to hold or toggle focus and adjust view. The
edges form a recognizable pair of diagonals, with the obelisk as a center reference. A non-color line assist
and caption confirm the geometry even in low graphics or a sandstorm. The focus completion registers
D40_F15 but the visible shimmer fades when focus ends. No cavern entrance opens until the complementary
observation is registered and safe interior collision is ready. The scout continues working, never declaring a
divine clue.

Scene 2 — Practice before the next march
Age eighteen. The corresponding escarpment shelters a supply camp. A collapsed pair of timber barricades
leans across the slope, their ends held by two stone supports. The obelisk remains recognizable against a
more damaged horizon. A quartermaster is supervising equipment repair while a tired trainee practices
ordinary footwork in a marked open space. The older hero may inspect his existing training equipment or
offer a simple demonstration without changing his persistent loadout.

Trainee: “When I turn, I lose the second blade.”


Hero: “Slow down. Remember where both of your hands are.”

Quartermaster: “A little practice now is better than learning during the next alarm.”

The older alignment uses the crossing barricade outlines and distant obelisk from a safe camp pad. The
player can register it before or after the younger alignment, during available playable migrations. Do not
force a migration, change inventory or automatically equip two blades. The trainee's practice is incidental
worldbuilding; the alignment is not gated by a tutorial or a combat minigame. A brief audio cue of two
wooden blades touching accompanies the success, with caption and visual equivalent.

Scene 3 — The cavern beneath the fins
Return to the younger ledge after both flags exist. While focus is active, a narrow seam becomes visible in the
cliff face. The seam opens into a recessed sandstone entrance with a broad threshold; do not show a
walkable opening before its collision and safe interior are loaded. Once inside, the player is protected by an
occupancy lease: loss of focus may hide the exterior shimmer, but it cannot remove the floor or trap the
player. If the player leaves before entering, the entrance can close without resetting the alignment.

The first room contains worn equipment niches, a fallen practice post and a low stone bench. On the left a
short passage crosses an old sand spill beneath a loose, hinged training screen. Its motion is telegraphed by
dust, an audible knock and a visible stop marker. Wait for it to settle or take the longer ramp to the right. The
bypass is always traversable by every weapon role, with no required dodge or damage. No unseen sand
collapse or instant-kill drop. At the end of both passages a shallow alcove contains a worn wooden case with
two training blades laid side by side. The case's edges are rubbed smooth by years of handling; the lining is
torn where the hilts rested. The player can inspect both before claiming the relic.

Hero: “I remember the weight of both.”

A short animation shows the younger hero lift the practice blades, check his grip, then lower them. The older
hero separately adjusts the grip of the equipment he already owns; he does not lose weapons or acquire
another set. The echo is bodily familiarity, not an immediate confirmation of their shared identity. The
proposed younger reward is a dual-blade technique if the game's existing role progression supports it. If that
technique is already unlocked, use an equivalent non-stat cosmetic or approved role reward. Never bypass
required progression, change a player's equipped weapon without consent, or create an unbalanced damage
multiplier. A practice post permits optional rehearsal with safe reset and no experience farming. The
discovery remains completable without doing a successful combo.

Scene 4 — A second view of the crossed blades
A passage beyond the case rises to a protected slit overlooking the desert. From this angle the fins align with
the obelisk differently: the crossed outline resolves into two parallel blades. Voluntary focus presents a
localized vista of the older supply camp, where the trainee returns the practice blades to a rack and the older
hero checks his own grip. This is a glimpse of an ordinary lived moment, not an instruction from a deity. The
younger hero hears a quiet impact of wood; the older hears the familiar creak of the case in memory. Neither
speaks the other's name. The journal records the sense of familiarity rather than claiming a complete
explanation.

The player may revisit the practice post and vista after claiming the reward. A visible mechanical latch opens
a safe return ramp to the shade shelter. The short route's screen resets once unoccupied, and the entrance


may be reopened by focus from outside. If the player logs out in the cave, restore the interior lease and exit;
if loading fails, restore the last safe exterior pad without resetting the claimed reward.

S26 — The Broken Akitsu Seal
R36 | Independent optional investigation. Catalog: recover a stolen diplomatic seal on the Akitsu road,
compare with register and establish forgery rather than guilt; age eighteen finds a seal-shaped evacuation
stamp; optional evidence for S29 and M14. S29 already expanded in R35 and requires any two validated
faction records. This entry defines the missing S26 source without altering the existing S29 evidence
threshold. May be completed before or after M14/M15 with appropriate dialogue, not required for main
progression. Proposed local characters, objects and geography below.

Scene 1 — The missing impression
Age fifteen. An administrative tent stands where the Akitsu road meets a neutral inspection lane. Papers are
kept under flat weights on a wooden table. A junior clerk has discovered that a diplomatic seal is missing, and
a messenger has delivered a disputed document bearing what looks like its impression. An officer is worried
that the document will be circulated as evidence of another empire's intentions. The officer asks the hero to


recover the original seal and compare the document with the register, rather than pursue a person based on
their clothing or affiliation.

Officer: “That seal proves nothing without the register.”

Hero: “Then show me the register.”

Officer: “Bring it intact.”

The player receives a local search area, not a named suspect. The clerk can explain where records were being
moved during the disruption, and a porter describes a damaged storage chest at a roadside depot. If the
assassination investigation has already concluded, the officer describes this as an unresolved archival
discrepancy rather than pretending the player is investigating an event that has not happened. A late
completion will append evidence to S29 where relevant, not rewrite M14's public statement.

Scene 2 — Recover what was misplaced
The depot is a small covered waystation with a broken latch and scattered shipping papers. The player can
ask a caretaker to open the gate, follow a broad service path, or inspect the damaged chest through the
available public access. No lockpicking skill, stealth build or combat is required. A small optional exterior
encounter may appear if the existing world encounter system authorizes it; it is not evidence of the forgery
and no faction affiliation changes because of it. The disputed seal is found in a marked storage box, and the
intact register is located in a second covered compartment. The player records their location and retrieves
them as quest-bound records, not saleable equipment. A caretaker may offer to carry the register if the
player is already escorting someone.

Caretaker: “They told us to get the papers under cover. We carried whatever boxes we could.”

Hero: “Did you see who put this here?”

Caretaker: “No. I saw the chest after the rain.”

The game records three separate observations: the recovered original, the intact register and the disputed
paper. The caretaker's report establishes only what they personally observed. No chase or confession is
forced. If the player exits mid-search, collected objects remain in the quest record and their props are
replaced by readable inspection markers rather than duplicated copies. The officer can accept partial
recovery and explain the missing item without completing the quest.

Scene 3 — A document that cannot be what it claims
At the tent the clerk prepares three enlarged fictional comparison cards. One shows the recovered seal's
registered impression; another shows the disputed impression; the third shows the ledger entry and its
authorized use. A simple in-game comparison highlights the inconsistent invented emblem and record ID.
The evidence establishes that the disputed diplomatic document is forged, not that the person who held or
transported the original committed the forgery. The comparison uses fictional records and abstract clues, not
real-world instructions for counterfeiting documents. Players can use a narrated description or accessible
text alternative instead of reading small textures or distinguishing colors.

Clerk: “This is the entry we have. The document claims another.”

Hero: “So it isn't what it says it is.”


Officer: “That is what we can establish. Not who made it.”

The player selects the supported conclusion. If they accuse an empire or the messenger, the officer explains
the missing connection and permits another attempt. There is no failure for being uncertain. The final report
separates the forgery finding from the unknown author and unknown commissioner. The officer returns the
original and register to accountable custody; the player keeps a journal reference, not the seal itself. They
can authorize a limited neutral summary for Sable Vey or keep it in official custody for a later private
submission. Either choice creates a validated S26 evidence record available through the existing S29 process,
subject to its actual access scope.

Scene 4 — The stamp of another emergency
Age eighteen. At an original-world evacuation office, the older hero sees a worn stamp used to record shelter
arrivals. The shape reminds him of the seal, but the records are ordinary wartime evacuation lists, not
diplomatic agreements. A registrar checks whether a family reached the receiving yard. The player can
inspect the stamp and a redacted ledger example, then assist in directing a waiting civilian to the correct
desk. The stamp remains in the registrar's possession. No identical emblem, shared faction or same author is
asserted.

Registrar: “The mark tells the next desk that someone checked the arrival.”

Hero: “A mark is only as useful as the record behind it.”

Registrar: “Exactly. Someone still has to read the record.”

This optional counterpart scene can occur in either order during normal transitions; it is recorded separately
from the younger investigation and is not needed to validate S26's local forgery. If the older visit is unfinished
when the younger quest completes, the journal retains the counterpart lead. Both observations teach the
importance of documentation without creating an impossible transfer of physical evidence.

Scene 5 — The record travels, not the accusation
On returning to the administrative tent, the player can see the original seal secured in a cabinet and the
register on the clerk's table. The officer thanks the hero for recovering the evidence before another
accusation could be based on it. If S29 is open, the validated source becomes available for comparison; if S29
is complete and this is its third valid source, the addendum path runs once. If S29 was completed with the
other two sources, do not replace its report or pay its completion reward again. No global claim that the
recovered seal prevented the war, and no confirmation of the hostile goddess or intermediary. Proposed
reward: ordinary optional XP, local trust and a journal record, once.
