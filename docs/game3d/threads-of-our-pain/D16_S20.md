# D16 — The Last Name / S20 — The Nameless Grave

Revision R19, 26 November 2026. Source: v1.4 R18, catalog paragraphs 551–556 and 906–910 (zero-based inspection). Preserve cemetery cave, spirit requesting name, old grave ledger, three-part seal, original burial, crypt side room, hero's memorial ribbon, optional spirit lore and line “Someone remembered.” Proposed supporting characters, personal names, local rituals and geometry are not final canon. S20 is an independent optional quest; D16 is a paired discovery, not a main-story gate.

## Scene 1 — A stone without a name (age 15)

Late autumn sunlight lies in narrow bands across a cemetery on a wooded slope. Wet leaves collect beneath worn headstones; the higher chapel has a roofless arch and small candle niches. The wind moves through the grass, but the sheltered entrance to the cemetery cave is almost silent. A keeper is cleaning a stone with a soft cloth rather than scraping away its surface. The player can hear a voice from the public cave alcove before accepting any objective.

SPIRIT [a distant, restrained voice, not a scream]: “Is there room for one more letter?”

KEEPER [stops cleaning, looks toward the cave]: “There it is again.”

HERO [turns toward the alcove, weapon lowered]: “Who said that?”

KEEPER: “I thought it was asking me to finish the stone. But there's nothing left to finish.”

The headstone has a shallow rectangular depression where a name tablet once rested. A small, illegible mark survives at the lower edge. The player inspects the stone without damaging it. In the cave a faint figure sits beside an empty candle niche, palms open on its knees. It does not advance or attack. The figure's face remains indistinct because the scene is about loss of identity, not because the player is expected to identify a hidden deity. Its visible outline and subtitles remain readable on low graphics.

SPIRIT [looks at the stone rather than the hero]: “I know somebody called me. I can't hear the end of it.”

HERO: “What do you remember?”

SPIRIT: “Rain. A door that wouldn't close. Someone waiting on the other side.”

Choice A, ask what happened: HERO: “Were you guarding the door?” SPIRIT: “Maybe. I remember keeping it open.”

Choice B, offer practical help: HERO: “We'll see if the keeper has a record.” SPIRIT [small nod]: “A record. Yes. Something that stays.”

Choice C, decline or leave: HERO: “I need to come back.” SPIRIT: “The stone isn't going anywhere.”

The keeper explains that the old ledger was moved from the chapel to a dry archive when its roof failed. S20 can begin from this conversation or from finding the ledger independently; the latter adds a journal observation rather than demanding that the player backtrack immediately. The protagonist believes he is dealing with a local spirit and an ordinary lost burial record. He has no proof of a previous lifetime.

## Scene 2 — A name worth writing down (age 18)

In the original world, the same ridge shelters a temporary burial ground. The chapel arch is intact here, but the graves are recent and the wind carries distant smoke. No gruesome imagery is needed: a shrouded defender rests beside the grave, their folded insignia placed on a cloth. Two exhausted survivors have stopped to assist. An older clerk holds a rain-stained register and a pencil with a broken tip. The hero already owns a personal memorial ribbon, tied to the inner strap of his pack; it is not the defender's burial ribbon.

CLERK [checks the register]: “The name on this list doesn't match the badge.”

SURVIVOR [voice strained]: “He had a nickname. Everybody used it.”

HERO: “What did his family call him?”

SURVIVOR [long pause]: “Taren. Taren Vale.”

Taren Vale is a provisional local NPC name, not an established mythological figure or a new identity for an existing character. Use a localization key and replace if owner approves another name. The older protagonist can help position the headstone, steady the ledger or carry a small lantern while the clerk records the burial. Each interaction reveals the same evidence; the game never assigns truth by arbitrary dialogue choice. The clerk makes a rectangular mark divided by a short diagonal stroke beside the name, signifying a confirmed entry. The player sees the clerk check it against the badge and the survivor's account. The mark is ordinary, not a magic rune.

CLERK: “I'll put the full name here. Somebody might come looking.”

HERO [steadies the page against the wind]: “Then leave space for them to write something.”

The player aligns the new headstone's corner, the upright register's divided mark, and the arch beyond. The older protagonist briefly hears the scrape of cloth against stone, although nobody is cleaning it. He checks the register and returns to the burial. Do not freeze the scene or require an involuntary camera pan. This is lived gameplay, not a narrated flashback.

## Scene 3 — S20: evidence before accusation

The younger player searches the public archive, reachable without paired focus. It contains a damaged ledger, a loose copy of a burial list and a keeper's rubbing of the old tablet. The final name is recovered by comparing three readable clues: the divided confirmation mark, matching grave location and a surviving family-name fragment. No particular order is necessary. A clue journal tracks evidence, not speculative conclusions. An accessible comparison mode lists the same clues in text. The keeper may explain how the records were indexed; this substitutes for fine visual matching but does not skip the evidence.

KEEPER [turns the loose copy over]: “This one was filed with the wrong row. See the corner mark?”

HERO: “The stone and the record have the same mark. And the name on the copy fits what's left.”

KEEPER: “Taren Vale. I'll read it out before I put it back.”

If the player guesses too early, the keeper responds: “Let's find something that tells us which grave.” No punishment, hostility or permanent incorrect inscription. If the player asks whether the spirit was a soldier, the keeper says: “The record says defender. It doesn't say whose uniform.” Keep faction and ancestral identity unresolved. The catalog's grief theme is expressed by giving a lost person a name, not by making a combatant out of a grieving spirit.

At the public alcove the player may speak the name privately, ask the keeper to witness, or leave the confirmed record and allow the keeper to read it. The spirit recognizes the name after hearing it or seeing the record; no demand that the player perform a religious rite.

HERO: “Taren Vale.”

SPIRIT [lifts its head, waits, then exhales]: “That sounds like something I'd turn around for.”

If accompanied by keeper: KEEPER: “I'll put it back on the stone.” SPIRIT: “Put it where somebody can see.”

If leaving record: SPIRIT: “Thank you for not guessing.”

The spirit becomes quieter and the keeper installs a replacement tablet after a natural reload or revisit. Whether the spirit departs permanently is unresolved; proposed final state is a quiet, optional conversation rather than a disappearance framed as proof of an afterlife system. S20 completes once identity is confirmed and the record is returned or shared. Proposed reward: local trust, modest experience and optional lore access, not a new combat power. S20 alone never grants the memorial ribbon.

## Scene 4 — The seal of three edges (D16)

A ledger facsimile is placed in an upright reading stand in the public chapel alcove. This clue exists whether S20 is accepted, completed or skipped. From a broad, stable viewpoint, the headstone's surviving corner, ledger symbol and distant chapel arch join into a complete seal. The player freely adjusts position and view; independent hold/toggle focus controls support both mouse and touch. Both reality alignment flags are required. Older alignment is available during the standard burial scene even if the optional quest is not accepted.

Once both are aligned, a narrow side opening in the crypt wall becomes visible. The hero can enter while focusing; losing focus before crossing hides it again. Threshold clearance preserves collision until the player reaches a safe interior checkpoint. A mundane interior latch and independent ascending corridor always allow exit. Do not trap the player behind the visual closure or reveal the hidden room on the public map before discovery.

HERO [brushes a hand along the opening]: “I looked here a minute ago.”

Inside, candle niches follow a gentle downward ramp. A junction offers a broad stone gallery and an optional narrower passage beside a tilting memorial slab. The slab is a damaged architectural element, not a magical punishment for disturbing graves. It visibly shifts, scrapes and releases dust before moving. A lever steadies it; the longer gallery avoids it entirely. No grave must be opened, no human remains looted. The optional alcove holds blank tablets and an old rubbing board, showing that this was a place where names were prepared for remembrance. The chamber beyond contains a folded memorial ribbon on a small shelf.

## Scene 5 — Someone remembered

The younger hero lifts the ribbon and notices a tiny fold in its edge. He turns it over, following the shape with his thumb. For an instant, the sound of rain on the chapel roof seems much closer than the quiet drip in the chamber. In the original world, the older protagonist stops beside his pack and touches his existing ribbon, surprised by the sudden feeling of having just received it. He tightens the knot gently and resumes his work. Do not duplicate or remove his inventory. The younger receives the memorial ribbon keepsake once; optional spirit lore may be unlocked through inspection, and S20 remains independent.

The second focus is an upper window overlooking the original grave row, arch and split ridge. In the younger reality the arch has lost its roof and the grave markers are eroded. In the original world the clerk reads the name aloud as a survivor prepares to leave.

SURVIVOR: “If his sister comes, will you tell her he stayed until we were through?”

CLERK: “I'll write it beside his name.”

HERO [holds down the page; after a pause]: “Write that he was waiting for her, too.”

The younger protagonist sees the register close. He looks down at the ribbon and says, softly, without looking directly at the camera: “Someone remembered.” The older feels the quiet of a long-abandoned chapel and turns toward the intact arch, momentarily unable to speak. Both return to ordinary control. Journal: “The same mark. The same name. The ribbon felt familiar.” No statement of reincarnation, literal historical continuity or a proven spirit identity across worlds. The defender's local story is not a clue that prematurely identifies the hostile goddess.
