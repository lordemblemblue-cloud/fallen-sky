## D14 — Scene 1: The wrong wing (age fifteen)

Morning rain has left pale streaks on the plaster of the artisan alley. Small wooden animals turn under a canopy, catching light along their painted edges. A bird hangs lower than the other toys because its left wing is shorter; the breeze makes it strike a wooden post twice, stop, then strike again. Let the player hear this before any objective appears. An artisan is using a thin sheet of wood as a template for a new bird. One wing on the template has an unmistakable angular join.

HERO (examining the template, not the toy): “Why is that one cut like that?”

ARTISAN (holds it against the new carving): “That's the way it comes. My grandfather knew that pattern.”

HERO: “But the wing was broken.”

ARTISAN (turns the template over): “Was it? Never seen it any other way.”

The boy has spoken with the certainty of someone correcting a practical mistake. His face changes when the artisan asks him how he knows. He offers no explanation. If the player chooses to examine the join, he traces a small offset with a fingertip. If the player asks about its age, the artisan says the oldest surviving pattern is several centuries old, though nobody knows who made it. If the player leaves, the toys remain in the alley; no quest timer starts.

Optional interaction with a hanging bird lets the player turn it gently at the display stand, exposing the underside. Do not add an inventory pickup here. The artist's hanging bird is a copy, not the discovery relic. The younger hero believes a craft tradition may explain the familiarity, but that explanation does not account for his knowledge of the damage.

## Scene 2: It just needs its wing (age eighteen / S17 encounter)

In the original world the corresponding terrace is a mining refuge. A temporary timber lean-to stands between the same rock shoulder and the same slanted ridge. Water drips from canvas into a dented bucket. A child sits on a crate trying to hold a broken wooden wing against a small bird. The caregiver is packing a blanket and asks the child to leave the toy behind if it cannot fit in the bundle. This is ordinary pressure of evacuation, not a villainous parent.

CHILD (looking at the join rather than the hero): “It doesn't need to fly. It just needs its wing.”

HERO (kneels beside the crate, leaves the weapon holstered): “I'll fix it.”

CAREGIVER: “We've got a few minutes. Don't make a job of it.”

HERO: “I won't.”

He puts a cloth on a spare board and offers it as a work surface. The child keeps the bird until the player asks permission. The repair takes three fictional interaction steps: match the two cut shapes, select the correct pre-cut wooden peg, and test that the wing sits flush. No real woodworking expertise or precision timing is necessary. A wrong selection makes the peg visibly fail to seat, and the child says, “That one's too fat.” The player can retry or ask the caregiver to help. The hero's familiar tools are already in his personal kit; the child owns this toy. No reward or inventory transfer of the child's property is implied.

If the player has not accepted S17, allow a brief observed repair in the older world's playable segment; the player may still focus on the repaired toy when it sits on the board. The full optional quest is only completed when the player explicitly helps and acknowledges the child. If the player walks away before the repair, the caregiver eventually sets the pieces aside for the local carpenter and the scene can be resumed; never manufacture a catastrophe for declining.

The child asks whether the repair will hold. The hero gently turns the bird over. “It won't be the same as before. But it won't fall off.” The child decides the join should remain visible and draws a short stripe on the wing. Later, with permission, the hero carves a separate smaller practice bird from a spare block, copying the same join and stripe. The child keeps the restored original; the hero keeps his little model. This second bird is the possession that corresponds to the younger relic. The distinction is mandatory for continuity.

The foreground bird supplies the body, the roof its extended wing, and the mural the short wing. The player may adjust position and view normally. ## Scene 4: What hands leave behind

The abandoned workshop is not a copy of the refuge. Its stone base and wood grain have weathered differently, and an older bench has survived in a recess protected from runoff. Sawdust is long gone; a thin layer of mineral grit covers the work surface. In one corner the player finds grooves worn into a board where successive workers held small objects. An optional drawer contains anonymous toy outlines that suggest the craft spread over time, without identifying who brought it here.

The chamber's main path passes a low rack of suspended wooden parts. The player may take a short route through a balanced rack, which tilts visibly and makes a dull clack when weight shifts. A nearby lever secures it, or a wider stone passage bypasses it entirely. The optional rack drops lightweight pieces into a catch tray, not onto the avatar; a reset allows retrieval. No mandatory enemy or damage. The room gives the player permission to move slowly and inspect objects without treating every old place as a combat dungeon.

At the protected bench, the younger protagonist finds a carved wooden bird beside a small set of old tools. Its short wing has the exact angular repair join and stripe. He turns it over, bringing the thumb to the edge before he looks at it.

HERO (a quiet involuntary laugh): “I fixed that wing.”

His expression tightens as he notices the tools are worn smooth and the pigment is almost gone. He checks the underside twice, looking for some explanation that could make the object recent. None is offered. The player claims the wooden bird keepsake and companion cosmetic once. The cosmetic should be a small carved-bird ornament on an approved companion attachment point, not a live bird, new combat pet or statistical advantage.

At age eighteen, the hero feels the small model already in his pocket and takes it out. His thumb finds the same join. The familiar object feels unexpectedly precious, as though he had only just finished carving it. He looks at the child, who is still playing with the separate original bird, then returns the model to his own pouch. No duplicate item, no confiscation, no disappearance. The two lives share the sensation, not a physical inventory transaction. Let the reaction play with a soft wooden click and a small smile rather than another line about the first time.

## Scene 5: The second focus — a bird that doesn't have to fly

A short stair climbs to a sheltered aperture above the workshop. The rock pier, roof silhouette and distant ridge notch align with the original refuge's bench and canvas opening. Only after claiming the relic can the player focus here. The younger sees the lived moment in which the older hero is sitting beside the child. The child has placed the repaired bird on a folded blanket and walks two fingers beneath its wings, pretending the blanket is a hill.

CHILD: “This is where it lives.”

HERO: “Looks like a good place.”

CHILD: “You can make another hill.”

The older hero folds a second corner of the blanket. He receives a sudden impression of sunlight on plaster and hears the hollow double knock of the hanging copy. He glances at the refuge's bare timber post and finds no hanging toys. The younger lowers his own bird to the sill and briefly runs two fingers alongside it. When focus ends, he leaves the bird in his pocket rather than throwing it or testing whether it flies. The feeling is tenderness, not a clue that the child is a specific known person. He may ask the artisan about the template again; the artisan admits the origin is uncertain. Journal: “The pattern is older than anyone here remembers. The join is the same.”
