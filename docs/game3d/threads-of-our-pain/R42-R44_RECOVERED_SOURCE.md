D46 — The Breath Beneath the Lake
R42 | Independent paired discovery | Source: original D46 catalog in R41, R41 opportunity manifest and
ending access policy. Preserve the three submerged stones, reflected air glyph, original dock supports, lake
cavern, visible air pockets, personal diving clasp and underwater traversal utility. All locations within the lake,
dialogue beyond the outline, dimensions, interactions and reward tuning below are proposed. Free-roam
discovery, not a prerequisite to any campaign chapter.

Scene 1 — A lake without a dock
The younger protagonist arrives along an established lakeside path, where the forest gives way to a shallow
shelf of pale stone. The water is still enough to reflect the trees, yet three dark stones below the surface are
positioned at different depths. From the ordinary approach they appear unremarkable; from a broad, dry
observation shelf their silhouettes and reflections combine into the shape of an air glyph. An old landing
notch in the rock suggests that somebody once used the shore, but it does not contain a physical dock. The
discrepancy is important: this landscape has its own history, and the protagonist's recollection is not
presented as proof that the younger world is false.

Hero (15): “There was a dock here.” He looks from the water to the empty bank. “Why do I remember
standing on it?”

The player may stop at the marked shore, turn toward the stones and activate the established focus
interaction. Do not force a cutscene, a swim, a precise reflection angle or a particular time of day. A subtle
contour shows the three relevant silhouettes when the player is inside the generous view volume. A low-
graphics rendering uses three static reflected outline proxies on the lake surface instead of expensive planar
reflections; these represent the same observation, not another puzzle. An accessibility interaction can align
the focus by inspecting the stones in sequence from the safe bank. Caption a soft chime, and provide a visible
shape response without requiring sound or color discrimination. Failed alignment leaves the player on stable
ground and allows another attempt.

An optional shoreline resident can explain that they have never seen a dock on this bank. This character has
no knowledge of the older protagonist and should not be written as a hidden witness to the invasion. A
weathered fishing basket and an empty mooring notch remain ordinary local details. The journal records the
observed shape and the protagonist's uncertain recollection separately. Players who have already
encountered M17 may receive a reflective line about correspondences, but no early-stage player is told the
hostile goddess's full plan.

Scene 2 — The supports in the original reality
At an authored age-eighteen opportunity, the older protagonist visits the corresponding dock during a safe
pause in the original world's evacuation logistics. The dock has been partly damaged, but its landward
platform is stable and three support beams remain identifiable. One beam bears an old waterline stain,
another is wrapped with rope, and the third has a small notch above the surface. Their projected
arrangement matches the air glyph without requiring the buildings or shore vegetation to match. The older
hero examines the supports from dry ground; an evacuation worker is checking a crate ledger nearby, with a
clear path inland.

Worker: “The far end is closed. Use the bank if you're heading back.”


Hero (18): “I remember diving from that side. Before the water got this high.”

The worker can respond that the old steps are no longer safe. The player looks across the three supports and
records FOCUS18. No enemy spawn or drowning hazard is attached to this observation, and the worker does
not ask the player to repair the dock as an artificial requirement. The hero's personal diving equipment
remains in his own life; a gesture toward an existing clasp or a remembered action is sufficient. Do not
duplicate inventory between realities. The observation can occur before or after FOCUS15, subject to the
authored opportunity manifest. If the original-world visit is not available yet, the journal marks it pending
without opening a manual world switch.

Scene 3 — The revealed entrance
Once both valid observations are recorded and the younger hero next approaches the shore, the outline of a
submerged stone lintel becomes visible. Its appearance should read as a previously concealed passage
resolving into view, rather than an instantaneous pit opening underneath the avatar. First show a narrow line
beneath the water; then, only after the entry, collision and return anchor are ready, show the physical arch
and a series of worn footholds leading down. The player chooses whether to enter. While the scene is
loading, maintain the original safe shoreline and do not disable ordinary movement. If loading fails, the arch
remains an outline and the focus pair remains saved.

There are two valid routes to the first chamber. The direct route crosses a short submerged throat with an
immediately visible air pocket at its far end; the alternate route follows a shallow sloping rock shelf around
the side of the arch and descends into the same chamber through a partly dry opening. Neither route
requires the reward inside, a particular weapon, an upgraded movement skill or high precision swimming.
The short swim is tuned against the weakest supported baseline breath and mobility settings, including a
conservative margin for turning and camera adjustment. If the game has no approved swimming system yet,
keep the accessible shallow route fully functional and defer the deep route rather than faking breath
mechanics or shipping a lethal volume.

Scene 4 — The cavern that held his breath
A chamber opens under a low dome of layered limestone. Its upper half is dry, and two distinct air pockets
are marked by pale mineral rings on the rock. Water laps against a narrow stone shelf; darkened lower
surfaces and pale dry surfaces communicate the waterline even when dynamic effects are disabled. A
submerged corridor leads to a second pool, where a visible shaft of light marks the next breathing space. The
main route stays on broad ledges and crosses water only where the exit is visible; optional submerged
alcoves contain nonessential environmental details, not required evidence.

The protagonist kneels beside a rust-stained storage niche and finds a personal diving clasp. Its brass frame is
dull, the leather loop has been repaired, and one edge has an asymmetric notch that his thumb finds without
looking. He adjusts it by habit and then stops, puzzled. The player can rotate the relic in the existing
inspection panel or read its description with a single interaction. Preserve the mystery of why his object
exists here; the clasp is a recognition event, not a mechanism that grants knowledge of all previous lives.

Hero (15): “I used to check this before I went under.” He turns it again. “I don't remember learning that.”

In the original reality a brief authored reaction, if available in the same opportunity or a later safe scene,
shows the older hero touching his own clasp while listening to water underneath the dock. It is a local
emotional correspondence, not an inventory transfer or unrestricted live communication. This reaction is


optional presentation, never a condition of the reward. Do not add it to a dangerous moment, interrupt a
different quest, or retroactively insert a new crossing after the final ending.

Scene 5 — An overlook beneath the water
Past the relic chamber is a dry side shelf facing a natural opening in the rock. From this second viewpoint,
three interior pillars frame a distant silhouette of the original dock. In high graphics the effect can be a
restrained static depth proxy with soft silver contours; on mobile it may be a simple sketch or authored
silhouette panel. No full second world, reflection scene camera or extra live NPC simulation is necessary. The
player can compare the two shoreline shapes and note that one dock exists in the original reality while no
corresponding structure stands in the younger world. This evidence stays local and does not reveal a new
history or antagonist identity.

The return path is a broad ramp leading back to the first air chamber and then to the shallow exit. A latch
may open an existing stone gate from inside, but never lock the player in. Once the discovery is unlocked and
the player has entered, keep access stable until they exit safely; visual closing or stream eviction must not
occur while an avatar occupies the chamber. The journal may offer a route marker back to shore, not an
instant teleporter across realities. Save checkpoints are dry and outside hazard volumes.

Underwater traversal and reward contract
Propose a bounded underwater traversal utility using existing approved movement and equipment systems.
Candidate effects are a small extension to ordinary breath capacity or improved swim handling in
exploration; choose one after the movement owner verifies a real supported statistic. No permanent
immunity, infinite sprint, PvP-only exploit, damage bonus, or mandatory equipment slot swap. The personal
diving clasp remains inspectable regardless of reward selection, and must not overwrite a currently equipped
item. If the movement feature is not implemented, issue the approved cosmetic or equivalent noncombat
utility instead, or keep the claim pending with clear communication; do not invent a hidden stat. If a utility is
already owned, offer the approved equivalent reward and retain equal quest XP.

If an oxygen meter already exists, expose it only during relevant submerged movement and show a non-
color-only low-breath cue. Entering a valid air space replenishes according to the existing rule; repeated
boundary crossing must not stack a buff. When the meter reaches its supported safety threshold, use the
approved rescue or environmental damage behavior with a valid dry checkpoint. A network disconnect or
unloaded floor never counts as successful exploration or intentional underwater failure. The reward cannot
be required to get back to shore. Do not implement separate breath timers in the HUD, physics and quest
scripts; a single authoritative movement state must drive their presentation.

S55 — A Place to Come Home To
R42 | Optional mountain sanctuary adventure | Source: S55 outline, D50 outline, M13 sanctuary, M18
deliberation, M19 ending contracts and M20 epilogues; R41 postgame audit. Preserve the younger hero's
garden before the final choice, older hero's shelter garden, seedlings vs mature garden, and reborn revisit
without explicit memories. Neither proposed ending is approved by this quest. D50 retains ownership of its
hidden passage, personal seed pouch and cosmetic reward. All NPC names/roles beyond existing source
roles, garden geometry and mechanics are proposals.

Scene 1 — A place that does not ask for a weapon
Before the final decision, the younger protagonist returns to the mountain sanctuary and sees a small
neglected terrace beside the existing refuge path. Its soil is compacted, a low retaining wall has a gap, and a
shallow channel once brought water down from the hillside. A gardener is clearing a handful of stones away
from the path. The character does not ask the protagonist to win a battle or prove allegiance to a deity; the
task is to help make a place where people can rest. Keep the damaged sanctuary and its established
monument intact. This is a small garden at an appropriate existing approach, not a replacement sanctuary or
another dungeon.

Gardener: “What should we plant?”

Hero (15): “Something that will outlive us.”

The gardener offers three supported plans: flowering plants beside the path, useful food or herbs in a shared
bed, or a mixture of both. These are cosmetic and community choices, not moral scores. All plans retain the
same usable paths, shared water access, rest bench and equal quest reward. A player may inspect a sketch
with captioned labels, or simply let the gardener choose. The selection is saved before any visual
construction changes. The protagonist does not need to buy rare seeds or own D50's personal pouch;
ordinary supplies belong to the sanctuary's existing store.

Scene 2 — Small acts of restoration
The player can help with three practical tasks: remove loose debris from the prepared bed, repair the little
water channel, and set a few seedlings in the ground. The first task is a single interaction per work area, with
an optional animation of lifting loose stones. The second uses a fitted channel piece and checks that the
water flows toward the bed, not the walkway. The third offers a low-effort placement mode with generous
snap points or an assist interaction that lets the gardener plant while the player observes. No real-world day
wait, paid currency, random seed drop, repeated watering grind or inventory material requirement. These
are authored set-piece tasks and may be completed in any order after the plan is selected, except water flow
validation precedes the final planted presentation.

Gardener: “There. Now the water knows where to go.”

Hero: “Do you think anyone will see it when it grows?”

Gardener: “Someone will. We don't have to know who.”

The camera stays with the player during work. Short animations use supported hand interactions, and a skip
applies the exact same committed result. The repaired channel has a quiet water sound and a visible trickle;


low graphics shows a static wet strip. The garden is initially young, with small leaves and bare soil between
them. Do not grow a mature garden in the same scene merely to signal reward. An optional conversation
allows the hero to rest on the bench or leave without further explanation. This scene is meant to be ordinary
life continuing despite the surrounding convergence crisis.

Scene 3 — The shelter garden in the original reality
At a supported age-eighteen visit, the older protagonist returns to the survivor shelter. A resident has
arranged a few containers near an existing light opening, but supplies and accessible floor space are limited.
The older hero helps choose a safe location that does not obstruct the evacuation route or consume essential
drinking water. The shelter garden is distinct from the younger hero's terrace: containers are made of
repaired crates and shallow pots rather than old stone beds. Both use similar planted arrangements, but the
matching shapes are emotional and visual parallels, not a direct exchange of resources.

Resident: “We have food for today. I wanted something for next week.”

Hero (18): “Then let's put it where the light reaches.”

The player can prepare two container positions, check the protected water allocation and add ordinary
seedlings from the shelter inventory. These supplies are scoped to the quest and may not consume resources
required for M18 or M19's evacuation. A resident may ask to keep one empty container ready for a future
plant; leaving that space is a valid intentional visual state. If an earlier shelter repair was skipped, use the safe
fallback alcove selected by the shelter owner, not an invented repair result. Do not spawn new survivors to
fill the scene or erase existing people. The garden stays small enough that residents can maintain it without
the hero's repeated presence.

The older hero can inspect a sprout and briefly rest. He does not speak to the younger hero through the plant
or recognize the younger protagonist's garden as an actual memory before the story permits it. This is an
independent local task in his own life. Persist the garden condition and selected plan separately from the
younger sanctuary. No mandatory paired-focus mechanic is attached to S55; D50 is a separate discovery with
its own observation records. Neither quest completes the other by implication.

Scene 4 — The future before the ending
Once the younger garden is restored, returning during the remaining pre-finale exploration shows the same
chosen layout with small changes appropriate to authored campaign phases: more leaves, a repaired wall, a
second watering pot, or a resident resting on the bench. Growth uses explicit stage transitions approved by
the quest owner, not the user's system clock or real-world waiting. An optional line reflects the progress of
the main campaign without suggesting that the garden has prevented the convergence. If the player never
starts S55, the sanctuary still has ordinary vegetation and a safe rest area. M19 and M20 must remain fully
playable.

The player can choose whether to leave a plain wooden marker with one of several short inscriptions or no
inscription. Options could include “For those who come after,” “May you find rest,” and an unmarked
marker. These are local cosmetic choices; they do not reveal player names in shared spaces, award faction
standing or alter the ending. Use localized fixed text, not an unmoderated public text input. If the player
declines, the garden remains complete and all rewards are available.


Scene 5 — A place to return to after the final choice
Path of Separation candidate: M20's younger-world epilogue can show the garden in a later growth state if

not depend on the optional task, and no character has to disappear to make plants grow. The original

Companion: “It feels peaceful here.”

Reborn protagonist: “It does. I think I'd like to stay a little longer.”

D47 — The Broken Clock
R43 | Independent paired discovery | Source: D47 original catalog, R41 opportunity manifest and R17–R20
campaign revelations (M17–M20). Preserve three shadow lines at a desert observatory, movable mirrors
operable at any time, evacuation signal dials in age eighteen, clockwork chamber, personal pocket dial and
evacuation log. Reward is time-anomaly lore, not literal time-stop. All new scene details, dimensions,
supporting roles and optional puzzle features below are proposals. No campaign prerequisite.

Scene 1 — The observatory without an hour
The younger protagonist follows an established desert trail to a weathered observatory. Its circular platform
is built on a sandstone shelf above the sand line. A cracked instrument pedestal stands at the center, with
three mirrors mounted at different heights along the perimeter. One mirror still has a brass adjustment
handle; another has a repaired wooden brace; the third is protected from wind by a short masonry wall. The
original builders intended to measure the passage of light across the sky, but the instrument has been
maintained unevenly. The player sees ordinary evidence of use rather than a room filled with inexplicable
magical machinery.

A local caretaker, if available in the current world phase, is cleaning sand from a shallow track near the
entrance. They explain that the old clock has not shown a reliable hour for years. They can identify the
handles as safe to turn but do not know what the three shadows mean. If the caretaker is absent because of
M14–M15 events, an engraved maintenance plate and the same interaction hints supply all necessary
information. Do not duplicate an NPC elsewhere or require the player to wait for a schedule.

Caretaker: “It never agrees with the sky anymore.”

Hero (15): “Then why are the mirrors still here?”

Caretaker: “Someone thought they were worth keeping.”

The player can walk around the platform, inspect any of the three handles and stand on a stable observation
slab. From there the circular markings look incomplete: three short lines have no apparent relationship until
the mirrors are turned. The journal distinguishes the observed clock-like markings from the hero's uncertain
interpretation. This is not evidence that time has literally stopped or that the younger life is an illusion. The
player may leave freely and return later, preserving any confirmed observation without preserving transient
animation poses.

Scene 2 — Aligning the three shadows
Each mirror has three coarse indexed positions and a small mechanical stop. Turning it plays a restrained
pivot animation and updates a projected guide on the platform. The middle position is not necessarily correct
for all three mirrors; choose visually distinct index notches and use an authored answer that can be
understood from the stone rim. A small triangular notch marks the origin of each shadow. When a mirror
reaches its correct position, its line meets the matching notch on the clock rim and a tactile click is captioned.
The final three lines form a dial with an asymmetric hand arrangement, allowing recognition without color or
sound.

The puzzle is deliberately independent of the current sky, weather, graphical quality or system clock. An
authored optical guide or low-cost shadow proxy provides the same lines at noon, dusk, night or during a


sandstorm. At night an existing instrument lamp can illuminate the projection; it is a local presentation
source rather than a requirement to acquire fuel. No costly additional shadow-casting lights or live raytracing
are required. The illusion of real optics should be supported by consistent pivot geometry, but the game uses
canonical puzzle state, not a numerical test of actual ray intersections. The player is never asked to
manipulate system time or wait for an astronomical event.

Provide an inspect-and-align alternative that lets the player select the matching notch from a single
accessible interaction. It must generate the same observation and rewards as rotating handles manually.
Keyboard, controller and touch should have predictable focus order, large target regions and no drag-only
requirement. While a handle is moving, accept at most one state change at a time and allow cancel to return
control safely; never leave the avatar locked in a rotation animation. Invalid combinations simply leave the
lines incomplete, without damage, currency cost or forced reset.

When all three indexed positions are validated and the hero is within the observation region, enable the
existing focus prompt. The player deliberately confirms FOCUS15. A brief silver contour joins the three marks,
then fades back into the platform. Avoid an intense full-screen flash. The journal records a dial-shaped
observation and, if the original-world opportunity has not yet occurred, marks the matching perspective as
pending. Do not give the player a manual realm-switch button or disclose M17 early.

Scene 3 — The instruments that guided an evacuation

Scene 1 — The letter at the first tower
