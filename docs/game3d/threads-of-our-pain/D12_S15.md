## Scene 1 — A watch with no one to relieve it (S15, age fifteen)

Dusk falls over the grass ridge. An old watchtower stands above the path, its pale stone darkened where rain has run down the mortar. The timber balcony has been patched with boards of different ages. Wind makes a loose shutter knock twice and then fall silent. From the road the hero can hear a small metal object roll across the floor, followed by a man's tired sigh. The keeper is kneeling next to a brass telescope, searching for the missing screw of its tripod. He has set a second stool near the instrument even though nobody else is there.

Keeper (without looking up): “If you're going upstairs, mind the third step. I've been meaning to mend it.”
Hero (picks up the screw, if the player chooses to help): “Is this yours?”
Keeper (holds out a hand, then squints at the screw): “That's the one. The thing's been slipping all week.”

If the player asks why he is still working after dark, he looks toward the ridge instead of answering immediately. “Used to have somebody to tell me when I'd missed something. Now I have to check twice.” Do not establish that the absent colleague died in an invasion; the keeper's loss or departure is intentionally local and unresolved. If the player wants to proceed quickly, he says, “Just hold the leg steady. I'll do the rest.” If the player walks past, the encounter remains available and the screw is returned to a visible catch tray. No timer or offscreen punishment.

The repair is a short physical interaction: seat the screw, tighten the brace, level the tripod. Each action changes the visible instrument; no inventory grind. Players can choose to manipulate the parts, ask the keeper to guide them, or use an assisted single interaction. The keeper steadies the tube while the hero adjusts the mount. His shoulders relax when the lens stops wobbling. S15 can also begin by examining the tripod before speaking to him. The repaired instrument remains usable after the quest. The hero regards the keeper as an ordinary person with a practical problem, not an oracle.

Keeper (peering through the lens, brow tightening): “The stars look wrong from here.”
Hero (noticing the three-point pattern): “Hold that angle.”
Keeper (pulls back and lets the hero look): “You see it too?”

The player sees three points above the ridge that form a narrow, asymmetric triangle. A nearby lens reflection and a notch in the parapet help identify the viewing angle. The telescope offers an optional magnified inspection, but alignment works with the normal camera at the tripod or an equivalent accessible stand. The keeper can see the anomalous pattern, not the older world or its memories. He suggests checking his old charts, then admits the cabinet upstairs is empty. The hero has no reason yet to identify the lights as anything other than unusual stars. If S15 is declined, the repaired or spare sighting frame still permits D12; use the keeper's assisted repair without granting quest completion.

## Scene 2 — The signal that shouldn't be there (age eighteen)

The corresponding ridge is under a cold overcast sky. The old tower in this reality is partly ruined, with a makeshift lookout platform behind a surviving wall. The silhouette of the ridge and its distinctive notch are identical, but the buildings are not. A lookout has been tracking lights that appear intermittently above the far ridge. The older hero carries a folded star chart, already owned and marked with his ordinary navigation notes. He uses the horizon to orient himself as he approaches the lookout; the chart is not a new reward.

Lookout (quietly, still watching): “There's another one. Higher than the first.”
Hero: “Is it ours?”
Lookout: “No. Ours answer from the road. Those aren't answering anything.”
Hero (sets the chart on a stone and shades it with his hand): “Keep looking. Tell me if the bottom one moves.”

The player can rotate a simple sighting frame on a stable platform, or align using the normal camera without touching equipment. Three signal lights resolve into the same asymmetric triangle. The lookout observes movement consistent with invaders, but cannot identify their origin or nature. Do not depict a specific hostile goddess or explain why these signals appear as stars in the preserved reality. The warning is actionable: the lookout will send word to the nearby evacuation party. There is no required combat, no target shooting and no unseen refugee death for missing the alignment. The hero briefly hears the gentle click of a telescope adjustment instead of the wind. He touches the folded edge of his chart, then resumes watching. He thinks exhaustion may have caused a momentary distraction. This is actual gameplay in his reality, not an imaginary cutscene.

Lookout: “You all right?”
Hero (after a short pause): “Yes. Mark the lower light before it goes.”

## Scene 3 — The stair behind the wall (D12 first focus)

The younger player returns to the watchtower when both reality alignment opportunities have occurred. The three stars are stable for the puzzle window; weather and day/night must not create a permanent gate. At the balcony the parapet notch and two lens guide marks frame the target. From the paired viewpoint the triangle resolves, and a dark seam appears beneath a seemingly solid rear wall. The old tower does not have to share the original tower's history; only its geographic sightlines are paired. A faint metallic tick, a high-contrast seam and optional controller vibration indicate the entrance is available. Keep the camera under player control. The player may cancel without losing a previously completed counterpart alignment.

Hero (soft, looking at the opening rather than at the keeper): “Those lights weren't stars.”
Keeper (turns toward him): “What did you say?”
Hero (hesitates): “Nothing. I think there's a stair.”

The line is an uncertain deduction from his lived experience, not a definitive explanation of the cosmos. The keeper may witness the doorway while the angle is aligned, but remains outside for safety. The player can descend only when both alignment flags and current focus are active. Losing focus before crossing makes the door appear closed; once the hero crosses the threshold, a stable interior checkpoint and independent exit route preserve access. No collision should disappear beneath the player or close across their body. The exterior visual closure can occur while the interior remains active. The keeper waits near the balcony and never blocks the entry.

## Scene 4 — Charts nobody finished

The staircase curves around a stone column. The air cools and the wind outside becomes a distant hum. Brass brackets, cracked slate panels and shallow niches suggest an observatory, though the preserved reality's builders are not identified. A narrow slit brings evening light to a landing; old floor marks show how a sighting frame once rotated. In the first gallery, three fixed chart fragments reproduce the triangle. One is an incomplete rubbing, one has a deliberately unfilled circle and one bears a different border style. This suggests repeated observation without proving who made the charts or what their observers knew. Optional inspection records sketches rather than a definitive chronology.

At the junction a worn counterweight support creaks as the player approaches. Its platform offers a shortcut to the chart room; a broad spiral passage reaches the same room without using it. On the optional route, a tilted plate and falling dust telegraph that the platform may settle. Wait until it stabilizes or use a lever to secure it. If the player slips, a lower catch landing returns them to the stair without lethal damage. The device resets when vacated or upon checkpoint reload. The longer passage has seats, handrails and no timed jumps. No reward or information essential to completion is locked behind the risky route.

At the far desk an old star chart lies in a protective sleeve. It has the same uneven triangle as the three lights, but no reliable dates or legible labels naming the invaders. The player can rotate the chart, place it under the fixed lens and inspect a plain fold that reveals a regional navigation clue. The clue points toward an existing ridge route to be surveyed by Claude, not a newly assigned coordinate or mandatory campaign objective. The hero folds the sheet in a practiced motion and pauses before sliding it away.

Hero (a small breath, surprised at the precision): “I knew where the crease was.”

At the next safe playable transition, the older protagonist retrieves the star chart he has carried all along. The texture of its fold suddenly feels unfamiliar and newly precious, as if he has only just received it. He presses it flat against the parapet while the lookout writes a message. The older keeps his item; no duplicate or inventory removal. The younger receives the catalog star chart cosmetic and navigation clue once. Do not add a combat modifier or grant Celestial Sight.

## Scene 5 — The second constellation (second focus)

A short ascending corridor leads to an open slit above the chart desk. The ridge notch, surviving stone upright and a lower saddle form the shared three-depth view. Focus now reveals the corresponding lookout post during a lived moment in the original world: the lookout is writing down a warning while the older protagonist holds the chart steady. The warning is not a new apocalypse prophecy; it is the immediate consequence of seeing the three signals. The player can remain looking as long as desired, then freely turn away.

Lookout (folding the message): “I'll send it down. They'll know which road to avoid.”
Older hero: “Tell them why. Don't just send them another order.”

The younger protagonist sees the message handed to a runner, then hears the old keeper's shutter knock twice. He looks at the sky and back at his chart. The older hero briefly feels the warmth of the repaired telescope's brass mount beneath his fingers; he flexes them and goes back to the lookout. Neither character sees the full other world continuously or receives an explanation. The younger hero's journal reads: “Three lights. Two views of the ridge. The chart marks the same shape.” Preserve the earlier evidence of different time, but do not use this discovery to solve it. The older's warning remains a local event rather than a scripted proof of future convergence.

## S15 — The Quiet Watch: return, choices and consequences

S15's repair and shared watch are its own objectives, independently of whether D12 is recovered. After the tripod is stable, the player can sit beside the keeper, inspect the second pattern together, and choose a response. Dialogue should feel like companionship rather than a quest summary.

If the player offers to stay: Keeper (pushes the second stool closer): “You don't have to say anything. It's a good view.” Hero: “I can stay a little longer.” Allow a voluntary short ambient watch, skippable instantly without penalty. If the player asks about the missing colleague: Keeper: “She was better at naming them than I ever was.” Hero: “Then you can tell me the ones you remember.” If the player is in a hurry: Keeper: “Go on. You've already done more than I expected.”

If D12 is completed, an optional later line becomes available: Hero: “I found a chart downstairs. Some of the marks match.” Keeper (careful, not frightened): “Then keep it somewhere dry. Paper never lasts as long as stone.” If D12 is not completed, his thanks are unchanged. The keeper resumes his watch at dusk; daytime he repairs shutters or sits inside. On return after completion, the second stool remains available as an environmental interaction. Proposed S15 reward: modest experience and a common repair material, not the star chart. No extra named quest or reputation system required. The older lookout's warning action is a parallel playable scene, not a requirement to finish S15.
